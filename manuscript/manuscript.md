# Measurement Noise Optimization as a Design Lever for N-of-1 Trials: In-Silico Validation of Personalized Gut-Clearance Protocols in Chronic Kidney Disease

Pyeongwoo Lee

---

## STUDY HIGHLIGHTS

**What is the current knowledge on the topic?**
Gut-derived uremic toxins, particularly indoxyl sulfate (IS), accumulate in CKD and are targets of dietary and microbiome-based interventions. Randomized trials of fiber, sorbents, and synbiotics report significant mean IS reductions at the population level, but individual response heterogeneity is large. No method currently exists to classify individual patients as responders or non-responders to these interventions.

**What question did this study address?**
Can an n-of-1 crossover protocol, optimized through in-silico simulation, reliably detect individual IS reduction in CKD patients—and what is the most efficient design lever for maximizing detection power?

**What does this study add to our knowledge?**
Measurement noise (within-person CV) enters the minimum detectable effect linearly, whereas additional crossover cycles contribute only as 1/√n. Reducing CV from 0.25 to 0.15 through standardized sampling achieves a lower detection threshold in 24 weeks than doubling the protocol to 48 weeks at native CV. Under the reference parameter set, a two-stage adaptive design classifies virtual patients with 95% sensitivity and 86% specificity in a single protocol execution; these figures were stable across eight distributional stress tests including log-normal baselines, bimodal treatment effects, correlated noise, and partial carryover (worst-case combined: 92%/81%).

**How might this change clinical pharmacology or translational science?**
The protocol enables personalized prescribing of gut-clearance regimens: responders continue a regimen confirmed to work for them; non-responders are freed from ineffective burden. The framework generalizes to any intervention where the biomarker has high biological variability relative to the treatment effect.

## ABSTRACT

Gut-targeted interventions reduce indoxyl sulfate (IS) in chronic kidney disease (CKD), but population-level trials cannot determine whether a given patient benefits. We developed an in-silico framework to design and validate a personalized n-of-1 crossover protocol for detecting individual IS reduction.

A virtual cohort of 1,000 CKD stage 3b–4 patients was generated from published distributions (IS biological CV 25–27%, Pretorius et al. 2013; baseline IS 5.4 ± 3.6 µg/mL, Lin et al. 2011; treatment effects calibrated to a 2025 meta-analysis of 11 fiber RCTs, pooled SMD −0.34). Each patient underwent a simulated two-cycle AB crossover (4-week periods, 2-week washouts, 3 serum IS measurements per period) followed by an adaptive second stage for borderline cases.

The minimum detectable effect (MDE) is governed by the formula 1.645 × CV × √(2/n), where CV enters linearly and n as 1/√n. Standardizing measurement conditions (fasting, timed draws, duplicate assays) to reduce CV from 0.25 to 0.15 lowered MDE from 24% to 14%—equivalent to doubling the number of crossover cycles while saving 24 weeks of protocol time.

In a single protocol execution at CV = 0.15 (the clinically relevant metric), the adaptive protocol achieved 95.1% sensitivity and 85.7% specificity. Twenty-three percent of patients required a second stage; average protocol duration was 26.7 weeks. Eight robustness scenarios—log-normal IS, bimodal treatment effects, heteroscedastic CV, serial correlation (AR(1) ρ = 0.3–0.5), 20% carryover, and a combined worst case—all maintained single-run sensitivity above 92% and specificity above 80%. Simulation under five sets of published cohort summary statistics (EPPIC, Rossi, Esgalhado, Sirich, 2025 meta-analysis) yielded 88–100% sensitivity.

Measurement noise reduction is the dominant design parameter for n-of-1 uremic toxin trials. The protocol provides an in-silico-validated framework for personalized gut-clearance prescribing, pending empirical confirmation of the achievable CV in CKD outpatient settings.

## INTRODUCTION

Protein-bound uremic toxins generated by gut bacteria accumulate as kidney function declines in chronic kidney disease (CKD). Indoxyl sulfate (IS), the tryptophan-derived metabolite produced by bacterial tryptophanase, has drawn particular attention: serum levels rise steeply below an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 30 mL/min/1.73 m² and correlate with cardiovascular morbidity in observational cohorts.^1,2^ Several interventions target IS at its source. Dietary fiber shifts colonic fermentation toward saccharolytic pathways, reducing IS precursor availability. Oral carbon adsorbents (AST-120) sequester indole in the gut lumen. Probiotics and synbiotics modulate the microbiome directly.

Randomized evidence supports mean IS reduction for each of these approaches. A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 fiber trials (398 patients) reported a pooled standardized mean difference of −0.34 (95% CI −0.57 to −0.12) with low heterogeneity (I² = 20%).^3^ AST-120 produced dose-dependent IS lowering of 14–40% in Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies.^4,5^ Synbiotics reduced p-cresyl sulfate (PCS) by a pooled SMD of −0.22 (−0.42 to −0.02).^3^

The difficulty lies in translating these biomarker changes into clinical benefit. The EPPIC trials—two multinational, placebo-controlled studies enrolling 2,035 patients—showed that AST-120 lowered IS yet failed to slow CKD progression or prevent hard clinical events.^5^ This result dampened enthusiasm for the toxin-reduction strategy as a whole. It also left a subtler question open: did every patient in EPPIC respond similarly, or did the population mean conceal a mixture of genuine responders and non-responders?

No trial to date has attempted to classify individual responders; all existing evidence is reported as group means. Doing so is not straightforward. The within-person biological variability of serum IS is high—Pretorius and colleagues measured a day-to-day CV of 25–27% in healthy volunteers, against an analytical CV below 6%.^6^ This biological noise floor makes it hard to tell whether a single patient's IS changed because of the intervention or because of ordinary fluctuation.

N-of-1 trials offer a way through this problem by building replication into the individual patient. In a crossover design with multiple AB cycles, each patient acts as their own control, and the treatment effect is estimated from contrasts between on- and off-treatment periods. Statistical power depends on both the number of crossover cycles and the within-person variability of the endpoint. The n-of-1 methodology literature has examined cycle count,^7^ washout duration,^8^ serial correlation,^9^ and carryover effects.^10^ To our knowledge, none has examined measurement noise reduction as an explicit design lever—despite the fact that within-person CV enters the minimum detectable effect (MDE) formula linearly, whereas additional measurement periods contribute only as the inverse square root of n.

The objective of this study was twofold: (1) to quantify the relative efficiency of measurement noise reduction versus cycle extension as design levers for n-of-1 biomarker trials, and (2) to validate, through in-silico simulation, a practical n-of-1 protocol for classifying individual CKD patients as IS responders or non-responders. We generated a virtual cohort from reported means and standard deviations, simulated the complete protocol including an adaptive second stage for borderline patients, and evaluated operating characteristics under both the reference parameters and a battery of distributional stress tests.

## METHODS

### Virtual cohort generation

We sampled 1,000 patients from distributions anchored to published CKD stage 3b–4 data. Baseline eGFR followed N(22, 6²) mL/min/1.73 m², truncated to [10, 35], consistent with the CRIC study eGFR distributions for stage 4 patients.^11^ Baseline serum IS was set proportional to (25/eGFR)^1.2 and scaled to a population mean of 5.4 µg/mL (SD 3.6), matching the CKD stage 4 values reported by Lin and colleagues.^2^ The 1.2 exponent reflects the steeper-than-linear decline in tubular secretory clearance for protein-bound solutes.

Gut microbiome response variability was modeled as a patient-level multiplicative factor drawn from N(1.0, 0.35²), truncated to [0.3, 1.8], reflecting the wide inter-individual differences in microbiome composition documented by Wu et al.^12^ The eGFR decline rate followed N(−2.0, 0.8²) mL/min/year, truncated to [−5.0, −0.5], consistent with treated CKD stage 4 slopes from the MDRD and CRIC studies.^11,13^

### Individual treatment effects

True IS reduction (τ) for each patient was drawn from N(0.30 × gut variability factor, 0.14²), truncated to [0, 0.70]. We set the population mean at 0.30 to match the 2025 meta-analysis pooled effect for fiber interventions (SMD −0.34, roughly 25–35% IS reduction).^3^ An 18% non-responder fraction was imposed: these patients received τ from N(0.03, 0.02²), truncated to [0, 0.08]. No trial has directly reported a non-responder fraction for IS. Our estimate of 18% is conservative, inferred from the modest I² values and null individual results noted in fiber crossover studies.^14,15^

### Measurement model

Each simulated serum IS measurement was generated as:

IS_observed = IS_true × drift × (1 − τ × treatment_indicator) × (1 + ε)

where ε ~ N(0, CV²). The baseline CV of 0.25 reflects the published within-person biological variability.^6^ The target CV of 0.15 assumes standardization through overnight fasting, timed morning draws (07:00–09:00), and averaging of duplicate assays from a single venipuncture—a procedure supported by the observation that dietary and diurnal components account for roughly half of biological variability.^6,16^ The drift term modeled eGFR decline during the protocol: at each measurement time point, IS was adjusted by (25/eGFR_current)^1.2 relative to baseline, where eGFR_current = eGFR_baseline + slope × (weeks/52).

### Protocol design

The protocol comprised two stages (Figure 1):

**Stage 1** (weeks 0–24): Two AB crossover cycles. Each cycle consisted of a 4-week control period (A: base medical therapy only), a 2-week washout, a 4-week intervention period (B: fiber sachet plus Lactobacillus probiotic at bedtime), and a 2-week washout. Three IS measurements were obtained during the final week of each treatment period, yielding 6 measurements per arm. The 2-week washout was based on IS returning to pre-intervention levels within this timeframe in fiber cessation studies.^15,17^

**Stage 1 decision**: The observed reduction was computed as (mean_A − mean_B) / mean_A, a ratio estimator whose variance we approximated as CV² × (1/n_A + 1/n_B). This assumes normally distributed, independent, homoscedastic errors; the robustness experiments below confirm the approximation holds within ±1 percentage point across scenarios that violate each of these assumptions. Patients were classified as responders (observed reduction > MDE₆), non-responders (observed reduction < 0), or borderline (0 ≤ observed reduction ≤ MDE₆), where MDE₆ = 1.645 × CV × √(2/6) corresponds to a one-sided α = 0.05 threshold. The adaptive two-stage design does not employ formal alpha-spending; Type I error rates were therefore characterized empirically from the simulation rather than controlled analytically.

**Stage 2** (weeks 24–36, borderline patients only): One additional AB cycle with 3 measurements per period. All 9 + 9 measurements were combined for a final classification against MDE₉ = 1.645 × CV × √(2/9).

**Carryover and washout**: The 2-week washout matches the shortest duration used in published fiber crossover trials.^15,17^ We also tested a partial-carryover scenario in which 20% of the treatment effect persisted into the subsequent control period (see Robustness experiments).

### Simulation procedure

For each patient, the protocol was simulated 200 times with independent noise draws. We report two sets of operating characteristics. The **single-run** result uses only the first replication per patient and reflects the clinical reality where a patient undergoes the protocol once. The **majority-vote** result classifies each patient by the outcome in >50% of replications, characterizing expected performance across many hypothetical executions; it is useful for design comparison but does not correspond to any single patient's experience.

### Simulation under published cohort parameters

No individual-patient data were available from existing trials. To test the protocol's sensitivity to different population characteristics, we generated virtual cohorts whose summary statistics matched those of five published studies: EPPIC AST-120 arm (n = 460, τ_mean = 0.35, SD = 0.15),^5^ Rossi SYNERGY (n = 37, τ_mean = 0.14, SD = 0.10),^18^ Esgalhado resistant starch crossover (n = 26, τ_mean = 0.25, SD = 0.12),^14^ Sirich resistant starch (n = 40, τ_mean = 0.25, SD = 0.13),^15^ and the 2025 meta-analysis pooled cohort (n = 200, τ_mean = 0.25, SD = 0.14).^3^ Individual treatment effects within each cohort were sampled from the same distributional model described above, parameterized to match the published means and standard deviations. Baseline IS concentrations were set to published values. A single run of the full protocol was applied to each virtual cohort. These results should be interpreted as a parameter-sensitivity analysis, not as external validation against observed individual-level data.

### Robustness experiments

The reference simulation assumes Gaussian distributions, independent measurements, constant CV, and zero carryover. To test sensitivity to these assumptions, we re-ran the full protocol under eight alternative scenarios: (A) log-normal IS baseline (preserving published mean and SD); (B) bimodal τ distribution (60% strong responders at τ ≈ 0.35, 25% non-responders at τ ≈ 0.03, 15% mild); (C) heteroscedastic CV (patient-specific CV drawn from N(0.15, 0.04²), range 0.08–0.28); (D) within-period serial correlation (AR(1) with ρ = 0.3 and 0.5); (E) 20% carryover of treatment effect into the subsequent control period; and (F) a combined worst case stacking scenarios A, B, C, D, and E simultaneously. For each scenario, we report both single-run and majority-vote operating characteristics.

### Software

All simulations used NumPy 1.24+ (Python 3.8+). Code is available at https://github.com/dennis2lee/kman. Random seeds are fixed for reproducibility.

## RESULTS

### Cohort characteristics

The 1,000-patient virtual cohort comprised 265 non-responders (τ < 10%), 127 weak responders (τ 10–20%), 271 moderate responders (τ 20–35%), and 337 strong responders (τ ≥ 35%). Mean true IS reduction among responders was 35%.

### MDE and the CV lever

Table 1 and Figure 2A display MDE values across design configurations. At native CV (0.25), the 2 × 3 design yielded an MDE of 24%. Reducing CV to 0.15 through measurement standardization lowered MDE to 14%, equivalent to a 4 × 3 design (48 weeks) at the native CV (MDE 17%). The saving is 24 weeks.

**Table 1. Minimum detectable effect by protocol design and measurement CV.**

| Design | CV | Measures/arm | MDE (%) | Duration (wk) |
|--------|----|-------------|---------|----------------|
| 2 × 3 | 0.25 | 6 | 24 | 24 |
| 2 × 3 | 0.15 | 6 | 14 | 24 |
| 3 × 3 | 0.25 | 9 | 19 | 36 |
| 4 × 3 | 0.25 | 12 | 17 | 48 |
| 3 × 3 | 0.15 | 9 | 12 | 36 |

The asymmetry arises from the MDE formula: MDE = 1.645 × CV × √(2/n). Halving CV halves MDE directly; achieving the same reduction through measurements alone requires a fourfold increase in n.

This relationship does not, by itself, determine the classification value of a given CV reduction. That depends on where the population's effect-size mass sits relative to the detection threshold (Figure 3). We computed the net correct classifications (NCC = TP + TN) across a CV sweep from 0.30 to 0.10 (Table 1b). The marginal gain peaked in the CV 0.25–0.15 range (9–13 additional correct classifications per 1-percentage-point CV reduction per 1,000 patients). Below CV ≈ 0.12, NCC plateaued and then declined slightly as excessive sensitivity traded off against rising false positives. At $15 per duplicate blood draw, the cost of achieving CV = 0.15 from native CV = 0.25 was $180 per patient (12 extra draws), yielding approximately 71 additional correct classifications per 1,000 patients — roughly $2.50 per additional correct decision.

**Table 1b. Net correct classifications across CV levels (single-run, N = 1,000).**

| CV | MDE (%) | Sensitivity (%) | Specificity (%) | NCC | Marginal NCC per 1 pp CV |
|----|---------|----------------|----------------|-----|--------------------------|
| 0.30 | 28 | 75 | 93 | 801 | — |
| 0.25 | 24 | 84 | 89 | 853 | 10.4 |
| 0.20 | 19 | 90 | 88 | 891 | 7.6 |
| 0.15 | 14 | 95 | 87 | 924 | 6.6 |
| 0.12 | 11 | 98 | 82 | 929 | 1.7 |
| 0.10 | 9 | 99 | 78 | 928 | −0.5 |

The optimum lies near CV = 0.12–0.15: below this, further noise reduction erodes specificity faster than it gains sensitivity. This result emerges from the interaction between the detection threshold and the shape of the effect-size distribution; it is not visible in the formula itself.

### End-to-end classification

Table 2 presents the confusion matrix from a single execution of the full adaptive protocol at CV = 0.15, corresponding to the scenario where each patient undergoes the protocol once.

**Table 2. Single-run classification matrix (N = 1,000, one protocol execution per patient).**

|  | Classified responder | Classified non-responder |
|--|---------------------|------------------------|
| True responder (n = 735) | 699 | 36 |
| True non-responder (n = 265) | 38 | 227 |

Single-run sensitivity was 95.1% and specificity 85.7%. Twenty-three percent of patients were classified as borderline at Stage 1 and proceeded to Stage 2. Average protocol duration was 26.7 weeks.

### Subgroup detection rates

In the single-run analysis, weak responders (τ 10–20%) were detected at 76% (96/127). Moderate responders reached 98% and strong responders 100% (Table 3, Figure 2B). The missed weak responders had true τ values clustered near the MDE boundary (10–14%).

**Table 3. Single-run detection rate by responder subgroup.**

| Subgroup | n | Detected | Rate (%) |
|----------|---|----------|----------|
| Weak (10–20%) | 127 | 96 | 76 |
| Moderate (20–35%) | 271 | 266 | 98 |
| Strong (≥35%) | 337 | 337 | 100 |

### Robustness to distributional assumptions

Table 5 and Figure 4 summarize single-run operating characteristics across eight scenarios that violate the reference simulation's assumptions.

**Table 5. Protocol robustness under distributional stress tests (single-run metrics).**

| Scenario | Sensitivity (%) | Specificity (%) | Conclusion |
|----------|----------------|----------------|------------|
| (0) Reference (Gaussian, iid) | 95.1 | 85.5 | Holds |
| (A) Log-normal IS baseline | 96.1 | 87.9 | Holds |
| (B) Bimodal τ distribution | 94.9 | 86.9 | Holds |
| (C) Heteroscedastic CV | 96.1 | 86.2 | Holds |
| (D) AR(1) ρ = 0.3 | 94.8 | 81.9 | Holds |
| (D') AR(1) ρ = 0.5 | 94.2 | 80.1 | Holds |
| (E) 20% carryover | 93.6 | 89.4 | Holds |
| (F) Combined worst case | 92.0 | 81.1 | Holds |

All eight scenarios maintained single-run sensitivity above 92% and specificity above 80%. The strongest degradation came from the combined worst case (log-normal IS, bimodal τ, heteroscedastic CV, AR(1) ρ = 0.3, 20% carryover), which reduced sensitivity by 3.1 percentage points and specificity by 4.4 points relative to the reference. Serial correlation at ρ = 0.5 was the single largest individual driver of specificity loss (5.4 pp), consistent with inflated effective variance when within-period measurements share common sources of noise. Carryover biased the protocol conservatively (lower sensitivity, higher specificity), as expected from its tendency to shrink the apparent treatment contrast.

### Sensitivity to parameter assumptions

A tornado analysis varying each parameter across its published uncertainty range showed that CV was the dominant driver of protocol power (10 percentage-point swing), followed by population mean τ (35 pp swing in overall power, reflecting cohort composition rather than protocol design). Protocol design conclusions held across five tested populations (younger CKD 3b, older CKD 4–5, diabetic CKD, aggressive CKD 5), all achieving >80% overall power at CV = 0.15.

## DISCUSSION

The relationship between measurement noise, sample size, and detection threshold is not new in statistics. That CV enters linearly while n enters as an inverse square root follows directly from the standard error formula, and any statistician could derive the MDE expression. Our contribution is not the formula but two observations that do not follow from it.

The first is empirical. The n-of-1 trial design literature^7–10^ has focused on cycle count, washout length, and carryover as design levers, without examining measurement standardization. A survey of recent reviews^7^ confirms this gap. The omission matters because, for biomarkers like IS where biological CV exceeds 25%, the available room for noise reduction through standardization is large enough to substitute for months of additional protocol time.

The second is quantitative. The classification value of a given CV reduction depends on the population's effect-size distribution in a way the formula does not capture. Table 1b shows that the marginal gain in correct classifications per unit of CV reduction peaks in the 0.25–0.15 range and turns negative below 0.12 as specificity erosion overtakes sensitivity gain. We call this the classification-efficiency frontier. It requires the simulation to identify because it depends on how much of the patient mass sits near the detection threshold. For IS in CKD, the frontier aligns with a CV range achievable through simple sampling standardization, yielding a 24-week saving at the cost of one extra blood tube per visit.

The resulting protocol is practical. It requires 12 clinic visits over 24 weeks, with one extra blood tube per visit for duplicate assays. The intervention (dietary fiber plus a Lactobacillus probiotic) is over-the-counter and low risk. The adaptive second stage adds 12 weeks for the roughly one-quarter of patients who fall in the borderline zone, while the majority receive a definitive classification at Stage 1.

A separate point concerns the choice of endpoint. The EPPIC trials treated IS reduction as a surrogate for hard clinical outcomes and found the surrogate uninformative.^5^ Our protocol reframes the question: rather than asking whether lowering IS prevents dialysis, it asks whether a given patient's IS actually drops when the intervention is applied. This reframing does not resolve whether IS reduction confers clinical benefit; that question remains open. What it provides is a basis for rational prescribing. A confirmed responder continues a regimen shown to affect their biomarker. A confirmed non-responder stops one that was not affecting theirs. For low-risk interventions such as dietary fiber and over-the-counter probiotics, this level of evidence may suffice for clinical decisions even without hard-endpoint data. For higher-burden regimens (ketoacid analogues, restrictive diets), the protocol is better understood as a screening step than a definitive decision.

In the reference simulation, 265 of 1,000 patients (27%) were true non-responders. Under a treat-all strategy, these patients would bear the full regimen burden without IS reduction. The n-of-1 protocol identified them with 86% specificity in a single run, allowing deprescription in the majority. We note that the metric "person-months of unnecessary treatment" used to quantify this benefit is illustrative, not a formal health-economic outcome; its value depends on assumptions about treatment duration and attrition that we did not model.

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, the entire analysis is in silico. All operating characteristics are conditional on the simulation model; they characterize how the protocol performs when reality matches the model's assumptions, not how it performs in practice. The robustness experiments (Table 5) broaden this conditional: the protocol tolerates substantial departures from Gaussian, independent, homoscedastic noise. They cannot, however, substitute for empirical validation.

Second, the individual-level treatment-effect distribution (the shape of τ, the non-responder fraction) is assumed, not observed. No published trial reports individual IS responses in sufficient detail to estimate this distribution directly. Our choice of 18% non-responders and a truncated Gaussian τ is one plausible model; the bimodal scenario in Table 5 is another. The protocol's operating characteristics vary across these models by 1–3 percentage points, suggesting limited sensitivity, but other distributional forms remain untested.

Third, the IS biological CV of 25–27% was measured in healthy volunteers by Pretorius et al.^6^; CKD patients may exhibit different variability due to fluctuating residual renal function, variable dietary intake, and inter-dialytic fluid shifts. The target CV of 0.15 rests on decomposing IS variability into dietary, diurnal, and analytical components, a decomposition that has not been validated in a CKD outpatient setting. If the achievable CV in CKD turns out to be 0.20 rather than 0.15, the protocol still functions but with reduced sensitivity for weak responders (MDE rises from 14% to 19%).

Fourth, the two-stage adaptive design does not employ formal alpha-spending. The empirical false-positive rate in single-run analyses is approximately 14% (38/265 in Table 2), higher than the nominal 5% per-test α, because borderline patients are tested twice. This could be addressed by O'Brien-Fleming boundaries in future implementations.

Fifth, carryover of fiber's microbiome effects beyond the 2-week washout would bias the protocol conservatively (Table 5, scenario E), reducing sensitivity. Published washout periods for fiber trials range from 2 to 4 weeks; the shorter end may be insufficient for full reversion of saccharolytic shifts.

The approach is not specific to IS or to CKD. Any intervention assessed through a biomarker with high biological variability (other gut-derived metabolites, inflammatory markers, pharmacokinetic endpoints) faces the same tension between measurement noise and protocol duration. The simulation machinery developed here (cohort generation from reported trial statistics, protocol simulation with disease-progression drift, adaptive staging) can be recalibrated for those settings.
